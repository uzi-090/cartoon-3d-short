# Story development

Use this before the narration recording. Respect the user's current phase: idea requests get brief descriptions, script requests get voice text, and storyboard artwork is optional.

## Ask useful questions

Use the conversation first. If the topic is open, ask about genre or offer a small varied set. If supplied, develop that premise. Establish the intended emotion and whether the story is fiction, a thought experiment or factual. Ask about language, approximate duration and audience only when the answer changes the work. Avoid a long questionnaire, repeated confirmations or proposing ideas after one has already been selected.

For an idea pitch, give an attention-catching opening, a few plain sentences showing the progression, and a concrete ending. Avoid confusing pitches that withhold the premise itself. Vary mechanisms and genres; do not turn every story into a two-choice contest, a scam or a joke.

## Work backward from a satisfying result

Name the question the opening raises. What does the protagonist want, what obstructs them, and what can change? In an explainer, the central question may replace a protagonist. Stakes can be curiosity, embarrassment, affection or discovery; arbitrary danger is unnecessary.

Explore the ending before polishing narration. It should fulfill the opening's promise through a discovery, consequence, success, failure, callback or reversal. A twist or moral is optional. Plant the details needed to understand the payoff. Do not invent a final rule or arriving character that explains everything without preparation.

For factual shorts, verify the event and causal claims using authoritative sources. Record supported facts, uncertain details and sources in `SCRIPT.md`; distinguish invented dialogue and dramatization. A reference video's account is not a factual authority. Do not turn disputed stories into confident history. Philosophical thought experiments should be labeled as such in the working brief.

## Test the causal outline

Write a short internal beat list: action/decision → result → what the viewer now knows or predicts. Each beat should change comprehension, expectation, stakes or the outcome. Combine or remove redundant beats. A sequence of new locations or inconveniences needs development rather than decoration.

Character choices, attempts and mistaken beliefs can generate consequences. A passive protagonist can work when evidence, costs or the causal mechanism unfolds meaningfully. Repetition earns its time when its meaning, scale, cost or response changes. Preserve enough uncertainty to invite prediction without hiding basic geography or a necessary rule.

Explain the story plainly without visual effects. If the hook, causal middle and payoff are still unclear, fix them before requesting a recording. Do not rely on impressive animation to conceal an empty middle.

## Write the voice text

Use conversational language, concrete verbs and varied short sentences. Start with an action or specific question. Dates, names, weather descriptions and introductions belong only where they aid comprehension. Dialogue needs a recognizable attitude and purpose. Avoid explaining the joke after its payoff or adding an unnecessary aftermath.

Assign information deliberately: narration supplies motives and connections; images can reveal evidence and discoveries. Leave time for viewers to notice them and see an identifiable character's reaction.

Use sparse, useful expression tags such as `[quietly, uncertain]` or `[with sudden confidence]` when appropriate for the user's voice tool. Separate clean narration from camera/SFX/staging notes so those notes are not spoken. Some TTS models read tags aloud; tell the user to use supported controls or remove unsupported tags.

Read aloud and check the internal visual beat list before handing off. Duration follows the material; do not pad to a minute or plan to accelerate the supplied voice later. Present the polished script and request the user's recording. Once supplied, preserve its words and pace unless the user asks for a revision.

## Learn without inventing performance claims

Use actual audience analytics when available. Compare the user's own videos of comparable length, distinguish initial interest from sustained attention, and treat dips as hypotheses rather than proof of their cause. Do not promise maximum views or universal viral thresholds. Recheck current official platform definitions when giving metric advice: [YouTube Shorts discovery](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en) and [performance definitions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en).
